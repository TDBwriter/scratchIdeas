# Sovereignty, the UN, and Neutrality

Companion to `infrastructureHubMission.md`, `infrastructureHubStabilization.md`, and `infrastructureHubEconomics.md`. This file has three goals:
- Keep Haiti sovereign, and keep the hub from being captured by any foreign power, donor, client, or domestic faction.
- Stay compliant with the UN and useful to it.
- Make the hub valuable to so many nations that keeping it safe is in everyone's interest.

**Bottom line:**
- **Sovereignty belongs to Haiti and its people, not to the hub.** For the hub, "independence" means it can't be captured. It never means independence from Haitian law. **The hub is independent of any government but anchored in the Haitian state** (Section 1).
- **The opposite failure is the blueprint's own "Moral Coup," and Haiti has already lived a version of it.**
  - Of $2.29B in aid in 2010–2011, less than 1% went through the Haitian government.
  - NGOs ran about 80% of social services.
  - Analysts warned of a "parallel state structure more powerful than the government itself."

  The hub must strengthen the state, not replace it.
- **Haiti's history shows what to avoid:**
  - **Debt.** France's 1825 independence "ransom" cost Haiti an estimated $21–115B in lost growth, according to the New York Times.
  - **Foreign control of revenue.** The US occupation of 1915–1934 took over Haiti's customs, and US financial control lasted until 1947.
  - **Unaccountable foreign presence.** The UN invoked immunity over the cholera epidemic linked to its peacekeepers, and its trust fund for victims raised about 2% of its goal.

  The plan avoids all three.
- **A cared-for populace won't care what its rulers are called.** Care is the foundation, and the People's Charter makes it durable (Section 12). Armed force can sit entirely under a UN mandate (Section 9), and distributed infrastructure gets built while the country waits for stability (Section 10).
- **Resilience comes from being useful to many and owned by none.** The model is SESAME, a research center in Jordan built under UNESCO, where Israel, Iran, Palestine, Turkey, and others sit on one council.

---

## 1. Legal status: anchored in the state, independent of any government

**The distinction.** The *government* is whoever holds office. The *state* is the permanent legal entity that signs treaties, grants land, licenses a reactor, and authorizes the use of force.
- The hub doesn't need any particular government to survive, and it never props one up.
- It does need the state.

### Why not bypass the government entirely?
The government's record really is that bad.
- Haiti has had no elected officials at all since January 2023, when the last ten senators' terms expired. The lower house had lapsed in 2020.
- The state couldn't protect its president, who was assassinated in 2021, or hold its capital.

But bypassing it doesn't work:
- **It has been tried.** After the 2010 earthquake, less than 1% of $2.29B in aid went through the government, and NGOs ran about 80% of social services. The result was a weaker state and a vacuum that gangs filled.
- **"No government role" isn't possible at this scale.** Once the hub runs water, power, transport, health, and security coordination for 3 million people, it is governing whether it claims the role or not. Governing without accountability is the blueprint's "Moral Coup."
- **Key parts of the hub legally require a state:**
  - **The reactor.** Only states join the NPT and sign IAEA safeguards agreements. Nuclear Suppliers Group rules require safeguards covering the whole recipient state before any fuel is supplied. Nuclear liability conventions run through the state where the reactor sits.
  - **Lethal force.** Without state and UN authority, Response Teams are a private army.
  - **Practical powers:** land, rights-of-way, port operation, customs, radio spectrum, cable landings, and courts for the strategy against gang leaders and their backers.
- **Legitimacy.** A foreign-funded body that moves in and ignores the state invites the charge of occupation, given Haiti's history, and gang leaders would exploit it.
- **A precedent for working around a captured government without replacing it** is Guatemala's CICIG, a UN-backed anti-impunity commission (2007–2019).
  - **What it achieved:** it prosecuted a sitting president, Otto Pérez Molina, who resigned in 2015 and was jailed, and it kept public support above 70%.
  - **How it ended:** it existed only by government consent. President Jimmy Morales shut it down in 2019, after his brother and son were indicted, calling it a violation of sovereignty.
  - **Lessons:** anchor the institution in a treaty that doesn't need renewing, build broad public support, and expect a backlash from elites.
- **The hub's best contribution to governance is training the next state.** The College's civic track produces the regulators, judges, customs officers, and administrators that Haiti lacks.

### Recommended structure: a treaty-anchored institution
- **Founded by an international agreement** between Haiti and partner states, in cooperation with UNESCO and the IAEA, on the SESAME and CERN model. Haiti is the host and a member, but never the owner.
- **A host-country agreement fixes what Haiti grants:**
  - Land and rights-of-way.
  - A nuclear regulator.
  - Authority to use force for Haitian-led Response Teams.
  - Customs and free-zone status.
  - Radio spectrum and cable landing rights.

  Under international law these commitments bind the state, so they survive changes of government.
- **Operationally independent:**
  - No government official sits on the hub's boards or directs its operations.
  - The hub pays nothing into government budgets and takes no money from them.
- **Haitian law still applies to ordinary matters.** The hub is not an enclave. The agreement also rules out foreign military bases and foreign control of customs.
- **No blanket immunity.** An independent claims tribunal hears complaints of harm caused by the hub (the cholera lesson).
- **Audits** by an international audit body and by Haiti's Superior Court of Auditors (CSCCA), all published on the ledger.
- **Parliamentary oversight** once Haiti has an elected parliament again.
- **Partners join through cooperation agreements:** UNESCO for the library and the College, the IAEA for the nuclear program, and WHO and PAHO for health.
- **No member, donor, or client holds a controlling share of anything.**

## 2. No single source of leverage

| Dependency | Risk | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| **Capital donors** | Strings attached; control | No donor provides more than 20% of capital or of the endowment. Gifts are unconditional, or their conditions are published. No donor gets a veto. |
| **Debt** | The 1825 lesson | No government-guaranteed debt for the hub. Donated capital only. |
| **Export clients** | Revenue leverage | No client provides more than 15% of revenue. |
| **Reactor fuel** | A supplier cuts off supply | Two qualified suppliers, neither under sanctions. The next reload is on site before each outage. |
| **Chips and electronics** | Export controls, cutoffs | Several vendors. Full compliance with export controls. The library's collections never depend on any one vendor's hardware. |
| **Solar panels and batteries** | Production concentrated in one country | At least two sources for each. |
| **The endowment** | One government freezes or seizes it | Held with several custodians in more than one neutral jurisdiction, under independent trustees and ethical investment rules. Norway's sovereign wealth fund ethics council is the model. |
| **The library** | Censorship or seizure | Mirrors in at least three jurisdictions. |
| **Security forces** | One foreign force dominating | Haitian-led, with UN-mandated partners only. Agreements governing foreign forces have time limits and a real way to claim compensation (the cholera lesson). |
| **Markets for Haitian goods** | Trade preferences lapse (HOPE/HELP lapsed in September 2025 before a retroactive renewal) | Sell more widely: CARICOM, Canada, the EU, Latin America, and Africa. |

## 3. UN compliance, built in

- **Sanctions** (the regime renewed by Resolution 2831 in October 2026):
  - Carry out asset freezes.
  - Check for arms-embargo violations at the North Campus port and on hub ships.
  - Cooperate on travel bans.
  - Share records with the UN Panel of Experts.
  - Keep the no-payment rule.
- **The IAEA:**
  - A comprehensive safeguards agreement plus the Additional Protocol.
  - Rescind the Small Quantities Protocol.
  - Before each nuclear phase, invite the IAEA's review missions:
    - **INIR**, which reviews the national nuclear infrastructure.
    - **IPPAS**, which reviews physical protection.
    - **OSART**, which reviews operational safety.
- **Human rights:**
  - UN human rights monitors (OHCHR and BINUH) get access to Response Team records and civilian-harm data.
  - The UN Basic Principles on the use of force apply.
  - Child-protection handover protocols are followed.
- **The Security Council mandate:** coordinate with the Gang Suppression Force under Resolution 2793 and whatever succeeds it, with Haiti in the lead.
- **Treaties:**
  - The nuclear non-proliferation, safety, and physical-protection treaties.
  - The anti-corruption conventions Haiti has ratified.
- **Reporting:** the hub's measures map to the UN Sustainable Development Goals for health (3), education (4), water (6), energy (7), infrastructure (9), and peace and justice (16). They feed Haiti's national reports to the UN.

## 4. Being useful to the UN and the world

- **Export Teams** become a resource for UN-backed reconstruction in other crisis cities.
- **A restraint training center.** UN peace operations and the countries that contribute police and troops train in the enemy-led-escalation doctrine and civilian-harm tracking.
- **From host to contributor.** Haitian police and units trained in restraint serve in UN missions abroad. Haiti goes from hosting UN missions to contributing to them.
- **A Caribbean disaster hub.** Free power, desalination, the port, the hospital, and the stockpiles serve the region after hurricanes, coordinated with CDEMA, the Caribbean disaster agency.
- **Public goods:**
  - The library: training data and evaluations in low-resource languages.
  - The Kreyòl language commons.
  - Open designs.
  - Medical isotopes for the Caribbean.
- **Neutral ground.** The hub hosts dialogues and training for parties who can't meet anywhere else, the way SESAME does.

## 5. Neutrality

- **Open to every nation on equal terms** for the library, training, and student places.
- **No exclusive alliances, no foreign military bases, and no role in any conflict.**
- **Security expertise is never exported as private military contracting.** It goes abroad only through UN-mandated missions, or as state-to-state training under UN norms. Haiti's experience with the contractor-supported drone campaign shows why.
- **Technology from more than one bloc,** with full compliance with every applicable export control.
- **One honest limit.** US export controls on advanced AI chips restrict who may use the compute. So "equal terms" applies fully to the library's collections and to training, but compute clients are limited to those the law allows.

## 6. If Haiti's government is overthrown

- **The hub never decides who governs.** It follows the recognition decisions of the UN and of the Organization of American States, whose Inter-American Democratic Charter Haiti has signed.
- **Lifeline services continue for people no matter what:** power, water, clinics, and school meals.
- **The endowment pays nothing to any authority that took power unconstitutionally,** as determined by the UN and the OAS. Its payments go directly to services instead.
- **The library's mirrors abroad keep the collection safe.**
- **Neither the Multi-Key Council nor the Response Teams has any role in a transfer of power.**

## 7. Why the world would want it to survive

- **Many countries have something at stake.** Partner nations each have students, data, mirrors, research, or regional services tied to the hub. As with CERN and SESAME, harming it harms many.
- **Its neighbors can see everything that matters.** The ledger shows money, the use of force, and customs.
- **Its contribution to peace can be measured:** civilian-harm data, defections from gangs, and regional disaster response.

## 8. Build order

- **Phase 0:**
  - Negotiate the founding international agreement and the host-country agreement with Haiti.
  - Set the donor caps and structure the endowment across jurisdictions.
  - Sign cooperation agreements with UNESCO and the IAEA.
  - Set up a sanctions-compliance unit.
- **Phases 1–2:**
  - Agree terms for foreign forces, including accountability.
  - Sign an agreement with CDEMA.
  - Welcome the first partner students.
- **Phases 3–4:**
  - Hold the IAEA review missions (INIR and IPPAS) before any nuclear work.
  - Open the restraint training center to UN contributors.
- **Phases 5–6:**
  - Haitian contributions to UN missions.
  - The regional disaster hub.
  - The neutral venue.

## 9. Force chartered under the UN

Putting the armed capability under a UN mandate, rather than owning it through the Haitian state, **strengthens the firewall.** The hub never owns force, and the mandate defines what force may do.

**Two routes:**
1. **A UN peacekeeping mission (blue helmets),** with Haitian-led units serving inside it. UN peacekeeping's own principles fit closely with the enemy-led-escalation rule:
   - Consent of the host.
   - Impartiality.
   - Force only in self-defense and in defense of the mandate.
2. **The current model:** a multinational force authorized by the Security Council, such as the Gang Suppression Force. It isn't UN peacekeeping, but it is backed by a UN support office.

**Requirements and limits:**
- **A Security Council vote,** which any of the five permanent members can veto. China and Russia abstained on the GSF.
- **Funding and troops.** Peacekeeping is paid for by assessed contributions from UN members, and countries have to volunteer troops.
- **Host consent.** Peacekeeping deploys at the invitation of a recognized national authority, which is another reason the state has to exist.
- **Accountability from day one.** MINUSTAH's legacy (cholera, and sexual exploitation by peacekeepers) makes blue helmets unpopular in Haiti. Any mission needs, from the start:
  - An agreement on the status of its forces that includes a real way to claim compensation.
  - A complaints channel for communities.
- **Haitian lead.** Units are led by Haitians, and there is no foreign command over Haitian streets.

## 10. Building while waiting for stability

The plan runs on two tracks.

| Start now: doesn't depend on stability | Wait: depends on stability |
|---|---|
| Solar canopies, microgrids, and batteries | Reactors (behind the nuclear gate) |
| Water kiosks and container sanitation | Renting data center capacity to clients, who need security and the subsea cable |
| Clinics with their own power | The rail spine through contested areas |
| School meals, the food reserve, and cold rooms | Heavy works at the North Campus port |
| The Kreyòl library and offline copies at stations | The cement plant and the steel mini-mill |
| Apprenticeships and Community Teams | |

- **The first track is small, spread out, and hard to capture.** Each piece is built only where Community Teams can work without an armed escort.
- **The first track also builds the people, the institutions, and the trust** that the second track needs.
- **Each gated item opens only when measured conditions are met:**
  - Security indicators in the area.
  - IAEA readiness for the nuclear phases.
  - Physical protection that meets the required standard.

## 11. On ending the Haitian state

**Four different things could be ended:**
1. The political class.
2. The government.
3. The constitution.
4. The state, meaning the legal entity itself.

The first three can be replaced. **Ending the fourth would cost ordinary Haitians the most:**
- **Nationality and passports** for about 11.7 million people and the diaspora.
- **Haiti's UN seat and voice.**
- **Membership in the NPT and the IAEA safeguards** the reactor depends on.
- **CARICOM membership and trade preferences** such as HOPE/HELP, which apply to Haiti specifically.
- **Treaties and maritime zones,** including fishing grounds and the seabed.
- **The legal basis for inviting a UN mission at all.**

**International law sets limits:**
- **The UN Charter (Article 78) rules out trusteeship over any UN member state.** The UN can't take Haiti over as a trust territory.
- **UN transitional administrations** (East Timor 1999–2002, and Kosovo from 1999) governed territories under Security Council authority. They did so as steps toward self-government, not to abolish a member state.

**The charter-city warning:**
- **Honduras created ZEDEs,** semi-autonomous zones that run under their own rules.
- **Then it reversed course.** It repealed the ZEDE law in 2022, and its Supreme Court reportedly declared the framework unconstitutional in 2024.
- **The Próspera zone's investors sued Honduras,** at one point for about $10.8B, and the case is still in international arbitration.

Zones that bypass national sovereignty provoke backlash and legal wars.

**What can be ended is the failed political order.**
- **Haitians can replace their constitution.** The transitional authorities tried to between 2021 and 2025, dropped the attempt in October 2025, and may be reviving it.
- **Haitians can elect new leaders,** with UN electoral assistance.
- **The state continues underneath as the legal shell** that holds nationality, the UN seat, and the treaties.

**The hub stays neutral about the form of government.** That decision belongs to Haitians, and the People's Charter holds under any form.

## 12. A cared-for populace

**The principle: a cared-for populace won't care what its rulers are called.** People judge whoever governs by daily life: power, water, clinics, food, schooling, and safety. Gangs know this too. The September 2025 drone strike in Simon Pelé hit a crowd gathered around a gang leader's gift distribution. Care buys loyalty, and the hub has to out-care everyone, reliably.

**The evidence adds three conditions.** Research on service delivery and state legitimacy in conflict-affected countries finds that services are **necessary but not sufficient.** Legitimacy depends on what is delivered and also on how.
1. **Fairness.** Care turns into legitimacy when it reaches everyone fairly. It turns into grievance when it follows contested lines: one neighborhood before another, or allies before rivals.
   - **Universal and unconditional lifeline services.**
   - **Spread small services everywhere early,** using the build-now track, instead of saturating a few areas.
   - **Publish the rollout order and the criteria for it** on the ledger.
2. **Dignity and voice.** People need to be treated with respect and to have a say: neighborhood seats on the council, anonymous complaints, and Kreyòl first. MINUSTAH also delivered services, but cholera and abuse without accountability destroyed the trust those services had earned.
3. **Reliability.** Taking away care that people have come to depend on turns gratitude into anger, as Haiti's 2018 fuel riots showed. That is why the guarantee reserve exists and why allowances only ever go up.

**One consequence to accept openly:** whoever delivers the care will be seen as the ruler in practice, whatever it is called. The guarantees below keep that power accountable.

### The People's Charter: guarantees that make care durable

These guarantees hold whatever the rulers are called. They are written into the founding treaty and the host-country agreement, enforced by the claims tribunal, and backed by mirrors abroad.

1. **Power and water.** Every household gets its lifeline supply, and it is never cut for political reasons.
2. **Health.** Clinics treat everyone by medical need alone.
3. **Learning.** Free school meals, access to schooling, and the library in Kreyòl.
4. **No conditions.** Food, water, health, and power are never tied to compliance or credits.
5. **Due process.** No one is harmed by armed forces outside the enemy-led-escalation rule. Complaints can be made anonymously and go to an independent tribunal.
6. **Privacy.** No tracking of anyone's movements, as little data collected as possible, and the firewall.
7. **Transparency.** The public ledger shows the money, every use of force, and customs.
8. **A way out.** Anyone leaving an armed group gets a path to a new life.
9. **A voice.** Neighborhood seats on the Multi-Key Council.
10. **Continuity.** The guarantees survive coups, new constitutions, and transitional administrations, and the endowment pays for services directly.
