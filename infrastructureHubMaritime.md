# Regional Shipping and Backhaul

Companion to `infrastructureHubSupplyChain.md`. This file covers two questions:
- Should goods move around the Caribbean and the wider Americas in unmanned submarines?
- What can Haiti send out so ships don't sail back empty?

**Bottom line:**
- **Use unmanned surface vessels, not cargo submarines.**
  - **Nobody operates commercial cargo submarines.** All the progress in autonomous shipping is on the surface. The IMO adopted its first safety code for autonomous surface ships in May 2026, and in January 2026 Japan put an automated coastal container ship into commercial service.
  - **In the Caribbean today, an unmanned vessel riding low in the water looks like a drug submarine.** In July 2025 Colombia seized the first unmanned "narco-sub," which was fitted with a Starlink antenna. The US military has carried out more than 70 lethal strikes on suspected drug boats since September 2025, including semi-submersibles.
- **The hub's ships should be the opposite of a narco-sub:**
  - Flagged and registered.
  - Broadcasting their position at all times.
  - Every cargo manifest published on the public ledger.
- **Ships will leave Haiti lighter than they arrive, whatever we do.** Haiti's trade deficit was about $3.9B in 2024, and apparel makes up most of its goods exports. The hub's biggest earners, data center access and expertise, don't need a ship at all. Outbound cargo comes from three sources:
  - Haiti's existing exports.
  - Kits the hub builds for partner sites.
  - Material that has to leave the island for recycling.

---

## 1. Why not cargo submarines

| Factor | Submarine | Autonomous surface vessel |
|---|---|---|
| **Commercial track record** | None for cargo | In service: Japan's GENBU coastal container ship and Norway's Yara Birkeland |
| **Rules** | No framework for unmanned cargo submarines | The IMO's code for autonomous surface ships has applied since 1 July 2026 (voluntary, with a mandatory version targeted for 2032) |
| **Cargo per cost** | Poor. A pressure hull carries little cargo for its size and uses more energy to move each tonne. | Good |
| **Navigation and communications** | Hard. GPS and radio don't work underwater, so the vessel must surface or trail an antenna. | Standard satellite and radio links |
| **Hurricanes** | Can dive below storm waves (its one real advantage) | Avoids storms by scheduling around them and sheltering in port |
| **How others see it** | **Looks like a drug-smuggling sub, and those are being struck on sight** | Visible and identifiable |
| **Capture risk** | If armed groups seize one, it is a ready-made smuggling platform | Little value to a smuggler once it is visible and tracked |

**Where underwater drones do belong:** close to home and for maintenance, not cargo:
- Inspecting the subsea cable.
- Maintaining the desalination intakes and the brine outfall.
- Surveying reefs and fish stocks.

They operate in Haitian waters, coordinated with the Haitian Coast Guard.

## 2. The fleet: transparent, remotely supervised surface vessels

- **Routes:**
  - **Local:** an electric autonomous ferry and cargo service across the bay to La Gonâve island, and coastal runs to Cap-Haïtien and the southern coast.
  - **Regional:** feeder runs to the big transshipment ports, including Kingston (Jamaica), Caucedo (Dominican Republic), and Miami. These are legs of a few hundred nautical miles.
- **Power:**
  - **Batteries** for the short local legs, charged with free power at the North Campus jetty.
  - **Longer legs** run on conventional fuel at first, then on synthetic fuels (e-methanol or ammonia) made with reactor power in Phase 5 and later.
- **Transparency instead of stealth:**
  - Flagged and registered with a national flag state.
  - Broadcasting their position over AIS, the standard ship-tracking system, at all times.
  - Every voyage filed in advance with the Haitian Coast Guard and with regional maritime authorities, including the US Coast Guard.
  - Every cargo manifest published on the public ledger.
  - Scanned at both ends.

  US law already makes it a crime to operate an *unflagged* submersible in international waters with intent to avoid detection. The hub does the reverse.
- **Control:**
  - A remote operations center at the North Campus, staffed by graduates.
  - Vessels start with reduced crews and move to remote supervision as the IMO code becomes mandatory.
- **Partners first.** Use existing feeder shipping lines before owning a whole fleet. Owned vessels fill the gaps.

## 3. What goes out so the trip isn't wasted

### Haiti's existing exports
- **Apparel.** Most of Haiti's goods exports; knit T-shirts and sweaters alone were about 55% of export value in 2023. US duty-free access under the HOPE/HELP programs runs through at least December 2026, and the Senate has passed an extension to 2028.
- **Vetiver oil.** Haiti supplies about half the world's supply, used in fine perfume.
- **Cocoa, coffee, and essential oils.**
- **Mangoes.** US imports have been suspended since October 2022 because inspectors couldn't work safely. They resume once inspections are possible again.
- **Rum, art, and crafts.**

### Products the hub makes
- **Kits for partner hubs,** shipped alongside the Export Teams:
  - Station canopy kits with racking fabricated in Haiti.
  - Sanitation containers molded from recycled plastic.
  - Assembled e-bikes.
  - Training materials.

  This is the mission's export model in physical form.
- **Processed foods:** chocolate and cocoa nibs, peanut butter, dried fruit, breadfruit flour, and fruit pulp, made using the cold rooms and dryers from the food plan.
- **Storage arrays for the library's partner mirrors.** They are light and valuable, and they ship on a regular schedule.
- **Cement, clinker, and rebar** surplus to Caribbean neighbors, once the local plants run (Phase 5 and later). These go by bulk carrier.

### Things that must leave the island
- **Used batteries** going back to recyclers under take-back contracts, packaged as dangerous goods.
- **Electronic waste,** worn-out solar panels and inverters, and server hardware.
- **Aluminum cans and any scrap or sorted plastic** beyond what local plants can use.
- **Empty containers** that need repositioning.

### Not by sea, or not on these vessels
- **Medical isotopes go by air.** Mo-99 loses about a fifth of its activity every day, so days at sea waste it.
- **Spent nuclear fuel never travels on an autonomous vessel.** It ships only on specialized vessels built under the IMO's code for irradiated nuclear fuel, under the supplier's take-back contract and with full security.
- **Expertise, data, and evaluation services** travel by air and fiber. They are the hub's biggest earners, and they weigh nothing.

### Filling ships both ways
- **Containers that come in** with food, spare parts, and equipment **go out** with apparel, vetiver, cocoa, kits, recyclables, and storage arrays.
- **Bulk cargo that comes in** (grain, and cement until local production starts) **goes out** later as clinker and scrap.
- **Triangle routes** such as Miami → North Campus → Kingston → Miami fill more space than out-and-back runs.

## 4. A new professional track

**Maritime operations:**
- Remote vessel operators.
- Marine engineers.
- Port and jetty operations.
- Underwater drone technicians.

This joins the engineering track at the College. **What Haiti can teach the world:** running transparent autonomous shipping for small island states.

## 5. Build order

- **Phases 1–2:**
  - Use existing feeder services.
  - Open the North Campus jetty.
  - Run an electric ferry and cargo service to La Gonâve, crewed and using automation aids.
  - Start underwater drone work on the cable and the intakes.
- **Phase 3:**
  - Remotely supervised coastal routes to Cap-Haïtien and the south coast.
  - The control center opens.
- **Phases 4–5:**
  - Regional autonomous feeders to Kingston, Caucedo, and Miami as the IMO code matures and port agreements are signed.
  - Synthetic fuel made with reactor power.
