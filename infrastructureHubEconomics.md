# Economics: Free Energy, Paid for by Export

Companion to `infrastructureHubSolarMobility.md`, `infrastructureHubLibrary.md`, and `infrastructureHubMission.md`.

**Direction:** energy is free to locals. The hub sustains itself entirely by exporting expertise and selling access to the data center.

**This file replaces two earlier money rules:**
- "Local revenue at cost" in the solar and mobility file, Section 1.
- "Partner fees cover cost only" in the mission file, Section 3.

**Bottom line:**
- **Free energy for locals can work.** At full build, the free allowances cost roughly **$60–100M a year** to supply. Mature export and data center revenue can plausibly cover that. The estimates and their assumptions are below.
- **"Free" means a generous guaranteed allowance, not unlimited use.** Unlimited free power invites waste and overloads the grid.
- **Self-sustainment is the end state, not the starting point.** Graduates and data center clients take years to arrive, so the grant and the endowment pay at first.
- **Never promise more free power than a reserve can guarantee.** In Haiti, withdrawing a subsidy has set off crises more than once:
  - In 2018, a fuel price increase triggered deadly riots, and the Prime Minister resigned.
  - In 2022, an announced fuel subsidy cut was the trigger for the G9 blockade of the Varreux fuel terminal.

## 1. What's free

| Who | What they get |
|---|---|
| **Every household** | About 150 kWh a month free: enough for lights, fans, a refrigerator, phones, a TV, and electric cooking |
| **Public services** (clinics, schools, water pumping, kiosks, street lighting, resource centers) | Free and unmetered, on priority circuits covered by the lifeline floors |
| **Small local businesses** (shops, workshops, moto-taxi charging) | A free business allowance, for example 300 kWh a month |
| **Public charging** | Free, with a cap per session so chargers are shared fairly. No account, no registration. |
| **Large commercial and industrial users** | Pay at cost above their business allowance, which covers their own load. **This is a choice.** Making them free too means export revenue must also cover their load. |

**Why an allowance rather than unlimited:**
- **Free and unlimited power invites waste.** In Punjab, India, free electricity for farm pumps has been linked to falling groundwater tables. And about 57% of EDH's energy never reaches a paying customer.
- **There is a precedent.** Since 2003, South Africa's Free Basic Electricity policy has given qualifying households 50 kWh a month. The hub's allowance is three times that.
- **Above the allowance,** a household can buy more at cost with prepaid tokens. Every home also keeps a guaranteed lifeline supply that is never cut off.
- **Meters enforce the allowance on the spot and report only monthly totals.** They don't record usage patterns, for the same privacy reasons as public charging.

**A co-benefit:** free power for cooking reduces demand for charcoal, which drives deforestation in Haiti. Less charcoal protects the Massif de la Selle watershed, the city's main water source, and cleans the air indoors.

## 2. What it costs

**Assumptions (full build, metro area):**
- About 3 million people in about 650,000 households.
- The household allowance totals about 1.2 TWh a year.
- Public services and small-business allowances add about 0.3–0.5 TWh a year.
- **Total free energy: about 1.5–1.7 TWh a year.**

**Cost to supply:** about $40–60 per MWh, covering operation, fuel, and replacement but not capital, which donors cover. For comparison, the US nuclear fleet's total generating cost was $31.76/MWh in 2023, including ongoing capital, fuel, and operations. Small reactors and the distribution network cost more.

**Total: roughly $60–100M a year at full build.** During the earlier solar-and-battery phases, with a smaller grid and smaller allowances, it is more like $10–30M a year.

## 3. What pays for it

**Illustrative revenue at maturity:**

| Source | Basis | Rough annual net |
|---|---|---|
| **Data center: colocation** (clients bring their own hardware; the hub sells space, power, cooling, and connectivity) | 30–60 MW at about $100/kW a month, priced below the US average of about $196 because the market is unproven | About $35–70M |
| **Data center: hosted computing and AI evaluation** (the hub owns the hardware) | Higher revenue, but the hub must replace its AI chips (GPUs) every 5–6 years, and estimates of revenue per megawatt vary widely | Highly variable; treat as upside |
| **Export Teams** | For example, 20 teams at $1.5–3M per team-year, paid by high-income clients | About $30–60M |
| **Partner students** | For example, 2,000 students at $15–30k each, paid by partner governments and sponsors | About $30–60M |
| **Endowment** | 4% of principal a year | Depends on size; a $2B endowment yields about $80M |

**Export revenue alone comes to roughly $95–190M a year, with the endowment as a backstop.** That would cover the free energy ($60–100M) and leave room for other free services, if the assumptions hold. These are planning estimates and need a proper financial model.

### Pricing by ability to pay
This is the "level playing field" applied to money.
- **High-income clients pay market rates:** frontier AI labs, wealthy governments, and multinationals.
- **Low-income partners and public-interest researchers pay cost or nothing.**
- **The library's collections stay free to everyone.** What the hub sells is space, power, computing, evaluation services, and expertise. There's no exclusive access, and no one can buy an evaluation score.
- **Any surplus funds free local services and the endowment.** Nothing is paid out to investors.

## 4. Risks and guardrails

- **Revenue arrives late.** Graduates take years to train. Clients won't place hardware in Port-au-Prince until the area is secure and the new subsea cable is in. Grants and the endowment fund free energy first, and export revenue takes over gradually.
- **A guarantee reserve.** Before launching any allowance level, hold a reserve covering about 5 years of its cost: roughly $300–500M at full build. Raise the allowance only after revenue has proven itself, and never lower it.
- **No client too big to lose.** Cap any single client at about 15% of revenue, and use multi-year contracts. Otherwise the largest AI client gains leverage over the city's power, a new Utility Valve held from outside Haiti.
- **Computing revenue is volatile.** GPU rental prices fall each time new chips arrive. GPUs last about 5–6 years; a reactor lasts about 60. The computing business must pay for its own hardware replacement before it funds anything else, which is why colocation is the base revenue.
- **Locals come before the data center.**
  - In a power shortage, the data center sheds load first, and client contracts say so up front.
  - The lifeline floors are never cut.
- **Export controls.** Advanced AI chips are subject to US export controls. Confirm the licensing requirements before planning hosted computing.

## 5. Build order

- **Phase 1:** public services get free power. A small household allowance runs on solar, funded by the grant.
- **Phases 2–3:** the allowance grows with the solar build-out and the grid. Colocation clients arrive once security and the subsea cable are in place.
- **Phases 4–5:** revenue from Export Teams and partner students begins, the reactors come online, and the allowance reaches full size.
- **Target:** by Phases 5–6, export and data center revenue covers free energy without drawing on the endowment's principal.
