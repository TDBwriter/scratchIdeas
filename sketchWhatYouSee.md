# Sketch What You See: Lesson Guide

The instructions for R2 (see `penPracticeRelease.md`). The book teaches one idea at a time, in order:

**lines → shapes → proportion → space → light → whole scenes**

Each lesson uses the skills before it, so nothing feels like a leap.

Reference pictures come from the pencil-sketch generator (`sketchArt/`): farmhouse, building fronts, city street, diner counter, nature. Real objects around you come first, though. The book is about learning to look.

---

## How every lesson works

**One habit runs through the whole book: Look → Measure → Mark → Check.**

1. **Look** longer than feels necessary, at the real thing, not your page.
2. **Measure** by eye: compare one part to another (taller, wider, steeper).
3. **Mark** lightly. First lines are guesses, so keep them faint.
4. **Check** against the real thing, fix, and only then go darker.

**Every lesson has the same parts:**

| Part | What it is |
|---|---|
| **You'll learn** | The one skill, in a sentence |
| **The idea** | What's going on, in plain words |
| **Do this** | Numbered steps |
| **Practice page** | Repetition with help that fades: **trace → dots → free** |
| **Draw from life** | A real object or scene to try it on (plus a generated reference if nothing's handy) |
| **Check yourself** | Questions to judge your own drawing. Circle your best attempt |
| **Common slips** | The usual mistake, and the fix |
| **Why it works** | The reason behind it (the "Why?" page) |

**Time**: 10–20 minutes per lesson. Repeat any lesson tomorrow on its repeat page with fresh targets. Repetition is the point.

**For kids**:
- Use the bigger-guide pages.
- Do half the cells.
- Skip "Measure" in Parts 1–2 and just enjoy looking.
- Parents: praise the *looking* ("you noticed the handle is lower!"), not just the result.

## Part 0: Start here

### S0.1 Set up to see
**You'll learn:** how to sit, hold the pen and look, so your hand can follow your eyes.

**The idea:** Most "I can't draw" comes from drawing what we *think* things look like (a symbol for "house") instead of what's actually in front of us. This book trains the switch from symbols to seeing.

**Do this:**
1. Sit so you can see your subject by moving only your eyes, not your whole head.
2. Hold the pen further back than for writing. Loose grip, light pressure.
3. **Move from the shoulder** for long lines, and from the elbow for medium ones. Save the wrist and fingers for small details.
4. Put the tablet flat or slightly tilted, and keep your page and subject in roughly the same line of sight.

**Practice page:** fill a box with slow loose loops, then a box of fast loose loops. Notice which looks livelier.

**Draw from life:** a 2-minute drawing of a cup, from memory. Then a 2-minute drawing of the same cup while looking at it. Keep both. You'll compare them on the last page of the book.

**Check yourself:** Where do the two drawings differ? That difference is what this book trains.

**Why it works:** A day-one "before" drawing makes progress visible, and visible progress keeps people practising.

## Part 1: Lines, the alphabet of drawing

### S1.1 Straight lines that go where you want
**You'll learn:** to draw a straight line from one point to another in a single confident stroke.

**The idea:** Slow lines wobble, because they follow every tremor of your hand. Quick, planned lines come out straighter.

**Do this:**
1. Look at the start dot, then the end dot.
2. **Ghost it:** move your pen over the path 2–3 times *without touching*.
3. Put the pen down on the start dot, and with your eyes on the **end dot**, draw in one smooth stroke.
4. Don't fix a bad line. Draw a new one next to it.

**Practice page:** 6 boxes of horizontal, vertical and diagonal lines (L1.01 in the sampler):
- boxes 1–2: dotted guides
- boxes 3–5: dots only
- box 6: match a given length freehand

**Draw from life:** the edges of a book, a door frame, a window. Draw only the straight edges.

**Check yourself:** Is the line straight? Did it land on the dot? Is it the same darkness from end to end?

**Common slips:**
- Short, hairy strokes. Fix: one stroke per line, and ghost first.
- Watching the pen tip. Fix: watch where you're going.

**Why it works:** Ghosting rehearses the movement, so the real stroke is a repeat rather than a guess.

### S1.2 Curves and arcs
**You'll learn:** smooth curves through three points, and S-curves through four.

**The idea:** A curve is still one planned movement. You just aim it through a middle point.

**Do this:**
1. Find the start, the middle (the top of the bump) and the end.
2. Ghost through all three. Your elbow does most of the turning.
3. Draw in one stroke. For an S-curve, feel the switch in direction at the middle.

**Practice page:**
- shallow and deep arcs through 3 dots
- S-curves through 4 dots
- free curves matching a given one

**Draw from life:** the rim of a bowl, a bent leaf, the curve of a chair back, a drooping cable.

**Check yourself:** Is the curve smooth, without flat spots or kinks? Does it pass through the middle point?

**Common slips:** a curve made of tiny straight bits. Fix: slow down the planning, then speed up the stroke.

**Why it works:** Drawings are built from just two marks, straights and curves. Control both and everything else is combination.

### S1.3 Seeing angles
**You'll learn:** to copy the exact slant of an edge.

**The idea:** We tend to "straighten" what we see, drawing slopes too flat or too steep. Comparing an angle to a vertical or horizontal makes it easy to judge.

**Do this:**
1. Hold your pen at arm's length, perfectly vertical or horizontal.
2. Line it up with the edge. How far off is the edge? Think **clock face**: "it points to 2 o'clock."
3. Draw a faint horizontal or vertical reference line on your page, then draw the edge at the same clock angle from it.

**Practice page:**
- copy angles shown next to each box (trace → protractor ticks → free)
- then "angle pairs": copy two edges that meet

**Draw from life:** a roof line, a staircase rail, a leaning broom, the slope of a laptop screen.
- *Reference:* `farmhouse` (roof slopes).

**Check yourself:** Hold the pen against your drawing at the same angle you held it against the real edge. Do they match?

**Common slips:** roofs drawn too steep. Fix: always compare to a horizontal before drawing.

**Why it works:** A vertical or horizontal reference turns a vague impression into something you can measure.

### S1.4 Following an edge: contour line
**You'll learn:** to draw the edges of an object by following them slowly with your eyes.

**The idea:** Imagine an ant crawling along the edge of the object. Your eye is the ant, and your pen moves at the same speed.

**Do this:**
1. Pick a starting point on an edge, and put your pen at the matching spot.
2. Move your eyes slowly along the edge, and let the pen copy the movement at the same speed.
3. Glance at the page only when you change direction or move to a new edge.
4. Include inside edges too (a fold, a seam, where the handle meets the cup).

**Practice page:**
- trace a contour of a simple object
- then follow it from dots placed at the corners
- then draw it from the reference alone

**Draw from life:** your non-drawing hand, a shoe, a crumpled paper bag.

**Check yourself:** Did you draw what you saw (odd bumps and all), or a tidy symbol of it?

**Common slips:** speeding up and inventing. Fix: if your eyes stopped, your pen should stop too.

**Why it works:** Matching eye speed to pen speed trains your hand to report what your eyes see. That's the core skill of observational drawing.

### S1.5 Blind contour (the most useful "bad" drawing)
**You'll learn:** to trust your eyes by drawing without looking at the page at all.

**The idea:** The result will look strange, and that's fine. This exercise isn't about the picture. It's about building the connection between eye and hand.

**Do this:**
1. Turn your body so you can't see your page (or cover your hand).
2. Draw your subject as a slow contour (S1.4) for 2 minutes **without looking down**.
3. Look at the result, laugh, and notice which parts are surprisingly accurate.

**Practice page:** 4 boxes of blind contours (hand, mug, plant, face in a mirror), each with a 2-minute timer box.

**Draw from life:** anything with interesting edges: keys, a sneaker, a houseplant.

**Check yourself:** Did you keep your eyes on the subject the whole time? That's the only score here.

**Why it works:** With the page hidden, you can't fall back on symbols. You have to look. After a few blind contours, normal contours get more accurate.

## Part 2: Shapes, seeing the simple inside the complex

### S2.1 The four basic shapes
**You'll learn:** to draw circles, squares, triangles and rectangles freehand, at any size.

**The idea:** Almost everything can be roughed in with these shapes. A circle is one continuous motion. Squares and triangles are straight lines that meet (S1.1 + S1.3).

**Do this:**
1. **Circles**: ghost a circle a few times, then draw in one or two passes. Start large.
2. **Squares and rectangles**: place the 4 corners as light dots first, then connect with straight strokes.
3. **Triangles**: 3 corner dots, then 3 lines. Check that the base is level.

**Practice page:**
- shapes in boxes, with guides fading from full to corner dots to free
- "match the size" shapes next to a given one

**Draw from life:**
- a clock (circle)
- a window (rectangle)
- a slice of pizza or a road sign (triangle)
- *Reference:* `buildingFront` is all rectangles.

**Check yourself:** Are circles round, not egg-shaped? Do squares have equal sides? Are rectangles the right width-to-height ratio?

**Common slips:** lopsided circles. Fix: draw them bigger, from the elbow, in one quick motion.

**Why it works:** Simple shapes are easy to get right. Getting the big simple shape right first stops small errors from piling up.

### S2.2 Ellipses: circles seen at an angle
**You'll learn:** to draw the oval a circle becomes when it tilts away from you (cup rims, plates, wheels, stool tops).

**The idea:**
- A circle seen at an angle is an **ellipse**: a perfectly symmetrical oval with rounded ends, never pointed.
- The flatter the angle, the thinner the ellipse.
- **Ellipses below your eye level get rounder the further below you they are.**

**Do this:**
1. Draw a faint horizontal line (the long axis) and a short vertical line through its middle (the short axis).
2. Ghost the ellipse around the cross, touching all four ends.
3. Draw in one smooth stroke. Keep the ends round.

**Practice page:**
- ellipses of increasing roundness (thin → round) on a shared axis
- stacked ellipses like a cup seen from above vs at eye level

**Draw from life:**
- a mug from the side, then from above
- a stack of plates
- *Reference:* `diner` (stool tops get rounder nearer the bottom of the picture).

**Check yourself:** Rounded ends, not points? Left and right halves the same? Lower ellipses rounder than higher ones?

**Common slips:** "lemon" ellipses with pointed ends. Fix: draw through the ends in one continuous loop.

**Why it works:** Cups, wheels, clocks, faces and the tops of cylinders all depend on ellipses. Learn them once and many objects get easier.

### S2.3 Seeing shapes in things
**You'll learn:** to break any object into a few basic shapes before drawing details.

**The idea:** A mug is a cylinder (two ellipses joined by straight sides) plus a loop for the handle. A farmhouse is a box plus a triangle. A person on a stool is an oval, a soft rectangle and a circle. **Start with the shapes, then add the details.**

**Do this:**
1. Squint, so details blur and big shapes remain.
2. Name the shapes out loud: "box, triangle, small box for the chimney."
3. Draw those shapes lightly, in the right places and sizes.
4. Only then draw the real edges on top, and add detail last.

**Practice page:** objects next to empty boxes:
- trace the shapes over a reference
- draw the shapes beside it
- then shapes and detail from the reference alone

**Draw from life:** a kettle, a lamp, a backpack, a car.
- *Reference:* `farmhouse` (box + triangle + small box), `diner` (people as oval + rectangle + circle).

**Check yourself:** Could someone recognise the object from your shapes alone, before any detail?

**Common slips:** starting with the eye of a face or a door handle. Fix: big shapes first, always.

**Why it works:** You can't place details correctly until the big shapes are right. This is how professionals start every drawing.

### S2.4 Negative space: draw the gaps
**You'll learn:** to draw the empty shapes between and around things.

**The idea:**
- Your brain has opinions about chairs and hands, but none about "the funny shape between the chair legs."
- Drawing the gaps sidesteps the brain's guesses, so the object comes out right by itself.

**Do this:**
1. Find an enclosed gap: between a cup and its handle, between the legs of a stool, between branches.
2. Draw only that gap's shape, carefully, as if it were the object.
3. Draw the neighbouring gaps. The object appears in the space between them.

**Practice page:**
- shade in the gaps of a simple object (trace)
- draw gaps from corner dots
- then draw an object only by its gaps

**Draw from life:** a chair, a bicycle, a plant against a window, your hand with fingers spread.
- *Reference:* `nature` (sky shapes between branches), `diner` (space between stools).

**Check yourself:** Do the gaps match the real gaps in shape and size?

**Why it works:** Gaps carry no labels, so you draw what you see. Accurate gaps force accurate objects.

## Part 3: Proportion and placement, getting sizes right

### S3.1 The envelope: box it in first
**You'll learn:** to start any drawing with a simple outer shape that sets its size and position on the page.

**The idea:**
- Before any edges, draw the **envelope**: a few straight lines that wrap the whole subject, like shrink-wrap.
- If the envelope is right, everything inside it will fit.

**Do this:**
1. Find the highest, lowest, leftmost and rightmost points of the subject.
2. Connect them with 4–8 straight lines that hug the outside.
3. Check the envelope's height against its width. Is it a tall shape or a wide one?
4. Inside, block in the main shapes (S2.3).

**Practice page:**
- references with the envelope drawn
- references with corner dots of the envelope
- references alone. Draw the envelope, then the subject

**Draw from life:** a houseplant, a sleeping pet, a bag on a chair.
- *Reference:* any generated scene.

**Check yourself:** Is your envelope the same shape (tall/wide ratio) as the real one?

**Common slips:** running off the page. Fix: the envelope comes first, so you see the fit before you commit.

**Why it works:** Placing the overall size and shape first prevents the most common beginner problem: the drawing growing as you go and not fitting the page.

### S3.2 Measuring with your pen
**You'll learn:** to compare sizes by measuring along your pen held at arm's length.

**The idea:** You don't need real units. Pick one part as your **unit** (the height of a cup, the width of a window) and compare everything else to it: "the door is two windows tall."

**Do this:**
1. Hold the pen at arm's length, **elbow locked**, one eye closed.
2. Line up the pen's tip with the top of your unit, and slide your thumb to mark the bottom.
3. Keep that measurement and compare: how many units tall is the whole object? How many wide?
4. Mark those proportions on your page with small ticks before drawing.

**Practice page:**
- references with a unit marked and a question ("how many units tall?")
- then measure and tick a full drawing

**Draw from life:** a doorway with a person in it, a bookshelf, a window and the wall around it.
- *Reference:* `buildingFront` (floor heights vs window heights).

**Check yourself:** Measure your drawing the same way. Are the ratios the same as the real thing?

**Common slips:** bending the elbow between measurements, which changes the scale. Fix: lock it every time.

**Why it works:** Your eye is good at comparing but bad at judging size in isolation. Measuring turns guesses into comparisons.

### S3.3 Lining things up: plumb lines and levels
**You'll learn:** to check what sits directly above, below or level with what.

**The idea:**
- Imagine a string hanging straight down from a point (a plumb line) or a ruler held perfectly level.
- What else does it touch? These alignments hold a drawing together.

**Do this:**
1. Hold your pen perfectly vertical against one point (say, the chimney).
2. Note what else lies on that vertical (it lines up with the left edge of the door).
3. Do the same horizontally (the top of the window is level with the bottom of the roof).
4. Draw these as faint guide lines and place parts along them.

**Practice page:** references with guide lines to trace, then to find yourself and mark.

**Draw from life:** a kitchen counter with objects, a car parked by a lamp post.
- *Reference:* `buildingFront` (window columns line up), `diner` (lamps line up with stools).

**Check yourself:** Pick three alignments in the real scene. Are they true in your drawing?

**Why it works:** Alignments are free accuracy checks. They catch errors that measuring misses.

### S3.4 The grid (training wheels)
**You'll learn:** to copy a picture accurately by breaking it into small squares, then do without the grid.

**The idea:** Copying one small square at a time is much easier than copying a whole picture. The grid teaches your eye what correct proportions feel like.

**Do this:**
1. On the practice page, a reference has a grid over it, and an empty grid sits beside it.
2. Copy what's in each square into the matching empty square, just the lines you see there.
3. Next page: the grid is lighter, only every second line.
4. Final page: no grid. Draw from the reference using only S3.1–S3.3.

**Practice page:** grid → half-grid → no grid, three times with different references.

**Draw from life:** hold a grid card or a window with panes in front of a scene. It works the same way.
- *Reference:* `farmhouse`, `cityStreet`.

**Check yourself:** Compare the no-grid drawing with the gridded one. How close did you get?

**Why it works:** The grid is help that fades, the same idea as trace → dots → free. It gives you the feeling of accuracy, then takes the support away.

## Part 4: Space and perspective, making it look deep

### S4.1 Eye level and the horizon
**You'll learn:** where your eye level is in a scene, and why it controls everything else.

**The idea:**
- The **horizon line** is always at **your eye level**, even indoors where you can't see it.
- Things above your eye level show their undersides. Things below show their tops.
- Lines going away from you slope **down** toward the horizon from above, and **up** toward it from below.

**Do this:**
1. Hold your pen level at eye height. That's your horizon. Draw it as a faint line across the page.
2. Notice: the ceiling lines slope down toward it, and the table edges slope up toward it.
3. Place objects by comparing to the horizon: "the top of the mailbox is below it, the roof is above it."

**Practice page:** scenes with the horizon to trace, then find and draw the horizon yourself on new references.

**Draw from life:** a hallway. Then the same hallway sitting on the floor (the horizon moves down with you).
- *Reference:* `diner` (lamps above, stools below), `cityStreet`.

**Check yourself:** Do all the lines going away from you head toward your horizon?

**Why it works:** One line on the page answers dozens of questions about which way edges should slope.

### S4.2 Depth without rules: overlap, size, height, detail
**You'll learn:** the four simple clues that make things look near or far.

**The idea:**
- **Overlap**: the thing in front hides part of the thing behind.
- **Size**: the same object looks smaller further away.
- **Height on the page**: below eye level, farther things sit higher.
- **Detail and darkness**: near things are sharper and darker. Far things are lighter and simpler.

**Do this:**
1. Draw three similar objects (trees, cups, people) at different distances, using all four clues.
2. Draw the nearest first, darkest and most detailed. Draw the farthest last, lightest and simplest.

**Practice page:** repeat a simple object at 4 distances along a road (with placement dots, then free).

**Draw from life:** cars along a street, trees along a field, chairs in a room.
- *Reference:* `nature` (far tree line is light and simple; foreground tree is dark and detailed), `farmhouse` (house vs barn vs silo).

**Check yourself:** Cover your drawing and point to what's nearest. Is it obvious?

**Why it works:** These clues work even without perspective rules, and they're how artists show deep space in landscapes.

### S4.3 One-point perspective: looking straight down a street
**You'll learn:** to draw a street, hallway or counter going straight away from you.

**The idea:** When you face straight down a street, all the lines that run away from you meet at **one point on the horizon**: the vanishing point. Vertical lines stay vertical. Horizontal lines across your view stay horizontal.

**Do this:**
1. Draw the horizon (S4.1) and put one vanishing point (VP) on it.
2. Draw the near edges of things (a building corner, the counter front) as plain rectangles.
3. From each corner, draw faint lines to the VP. These set the sides going away.
4. Cut them off with verticals where each building or object ends.
5. Windows, doors and stools follow the same lines to the VP. They get smaller and closer together as they go back.

**Practice page:**
- boxes floating above, on and below the horizon, connected to a VP (trace → corner dots → free)
- then a street built from boxes

**Draw from life:** a hallway, a train platform, a long table, a straight road.
- *Reference:* `cityStreet`, `diner`. A guide version of each page can show the horizon and VP faintly over the sketch.

**Check yourself:** Extend a few lines in your drawing. Do they meet at one point on the horizon?

**Common slips:** lines that miss the VP. Fix: draw the VP first and always aim at it.

**Why it works:** A single rule, applied every time, gives convincing depth. It's also the easiest perspective to see in real life.

### S4.4 Two-point perspective: looking at a corner
**You'll learn:** to draw a building or box seen corner-on.

**The idea:** When you see the corner of a box, its two sides run away in two directions, toward **two vanishing points** far apart on the horizon. Vertical edges stay vertical.

**Do this:**
1. Draw the horizon and place two VPs far apart, often off the page edges (mark them with a tick).
2. Draw the nearest vertical corner.
3. From its top and bottom, draw lines to the left VP and the right VP.
4. Cut the sides off with verticals. Join the back corners back to the opposite VPs if you need the top.
5. Add a triangle for a roof: find the middle of the end wall with an X from corner to corner, and put the peak above it.

**Practice page:**
- boxes in 2-point at different heights (trace → corner dots → free)
- then a simple house: box + roof

**Draw from life:** the corner of a building, a cereal box on a table, a shed.
- *Reference:* `farmhouse` (house and barn).

**Check yourself:** Do your verticals stay vertical? Do the receding lines meet at the two VPs?

**Common slips:**
- VPs too close together, so the box looks distorted. Fix: spread them wide.
- Finding the roof peak by eye. Fix: always use the X trick to find the centre.

**Why it works:** Most buildings are seen corner-on. Two-point perspective is the key to houses, streets and furniture.

### S4.5 Everything is boxes and cylinders
**You'll learn:** to build complicated objects from boxes and cylinders in perspective.

**The idea:**
- A car is two boxes and four ellipses. A stool is an ellipse, a line and a ring. A coffee machine is a box with a dome. A silo is a cylinder with a cap.
- Get the boxes and cylinders right in perspective, then carve and add.

**Do this:**
1. Find the main box or cylinder (the envelope in 3D).
2. Draw it in perspective (S4.3–S4.4), with ellipses (S2.2) for round parts.
3. Add the secondary boxes and cylinders: the cabin on the car, the handle on the mug.
4. Erase or lighten construction lines, and strengthen the real edges.

**Practice page:**
- objects shown as boxes and cylinders over a reference (trace)
- then as box dots
- then free, for a car, stool, mug and lamp

**Draw from life:** a toaster, a car, a lamp, a bin.
- *Reference:* `cityStreet` (cars), `diner` (stools, coffee machine, pie case), `farmhouse` (silo).

**Check yourself:** Are the ellipses of a cylinder lined up and rounder lower down (below eye level)?

**Why it works:** Boxes and cylinders are easy to put in perspective. Anything built from them inherits the correct perspective.

## Part 5: Light and value, making it look solid

### S5.1 A five-step value scale
**You'll learn:** to make five even steps from white to near-black with hatching.

**The idea:** **Value** means how light or dark something is. With a pen, darkness comes from how close the lines are and how many layers you add, not from pressing harder.

**Do this:**
1. Draw a bar of 5 boxes.
2. Box 1: leave white. Box 2: widely spaced parallel lines. Box 3: closer lines.
3. Box 4: add a second layer at an angle (cross-hatching). Box 5: a third layer, tight.
4. Squint. Are the steps even, or is there a big jump somewhere?

**Practice page:** value bars:
- trace a sample
- match a sample's grays
- then make your own scales with straight hatching, cross-hatching and curved hatching

**Draw from life:** a plain mug by a window: shade its light side, middle and dark side using three of your five values.

**Check yourself:** Five clear, even steps? Lines evenly spaced and parallel within each box?

**Common slips:** scribbly, uneven hatching. Fix: hatch with the same quick confident strokes as S1.1.

**Why it works:** A drawing with a full range of values looks solid and alive. A drawing using only middle grays looks flat.

### S5.2 Light on simple forms
**You'll learn:** how light falls on a box, a cylinder and a ball.

**The idea:**
- Every lit object has a **light side**, a **shadow side** and a **cast shadow** on the ground.
- On a box the change is sudden at each edge. On a cylinder or ball it's gradual.
- Later add the **reflected light** (a slightly lighter edge inside the shadow) and the **core shadow** (the darkest band where light turns to shadow).

**Do this:**
1. Decide where the light comes from and draw a small arrow.
2. **Box**: lightest top or side toward the light, middle side, darkest side away.
3. **Cylinder**: light band, core shadow band, slightly lighter reflected edge.
4. **Ball**: highlight, gradual turn into the core shadow, reflected light, then a cast shadow pointing away from the light.

**Practice page:** a box, cylinder and ball, each lit from three directions (shading guides traced → zone outlines only → free).

**Draw from life:** an orange and a box under one desk lamp in a dim room.
- *Reference:* `farmhouse` (shaded sides of house and barn), `nature` (rocks).

**Check yourself:**
- Does every object agree about where the light comes from?
- Do the cast shadows point away from it?

**Why it works:** Most objects are combinations of boxes, cylinders and balls. Learn the light on these three and you can shade almost anything.

### S5.3 Squint and group: shadow shapes
**You'll learn:** to see shadows as a few big shapes instead of lots of little ones.

**The idea:**
- Squinting blurs detail and shows the big light and dark masses.
- Draw those masses as shapes, using 2–3 values only, before any detail.

**Do this:**
1. Squint until the scene becomes a few blobs of light, middle and dark.
2. Outline the dark shapes lightly. Treat them like negative space (S2.4): they're just shapes.
3. Fill the darks with one even hatch, then the middles with lighter hatching. Leave the lights alone.
4. Only now add the few details that matter.

**Practice page:**
- references next to "3-value" versions (trace)
- then reduce new references to 3 values yourself, in small thumbnail boxes

**Draw from life:** a street at late afternoon, a room with one window, a face lit from the side.
- *Reference:* `cityStreet` (one side of the street in shadow).

**Check yourself:** Squint at your drawing and the scene. Do the big darks sit in the same places?

**Common slips:** shading every little thing separately. Fix: group first, details last.

**Why it works:** Our eyes read a picture by its big value shapes first. Getting those right does most of the work.

### S5.4 Texture and edges
**You'll learn:** to suggest surfaces (foliage, brick, glass, grass, water) without drawing every leaf or brick.

**The idea:**
- **Suggest, don't list.** A few bricks at the edges of a wall imply the whole wall.
- A loose looping scribble reads as leaves.
- A few diagonal strokes read as glass.
- Short horizontal dashes read as water.
- Edges matter too: **hard edges** come forward and **soft or broken edges** fall back.

**Do this:**
1. Pick a texture and make its "mark": loops for leaves, short dashes for bricks, diagonals for glass, ticks for grass, horizontal dashes for water.
2. Use it where the light changes or at the edges of the area, and leave the middle mostly empty.
3. Make near textures larger and darker, and far textures smaller and lighter (S4.2).

**Practice page:** texture swatches (trace → copy → invent), then a box filled with "a wall with a window" using suggestion only.

**Draw from life:** a tree, a brick wall, a shop window, a pond.
- *Reference:* `nature` (foliage, water), `buildingFront` (brick, glass).

**Check yourself:** Does the texture say "brick" or "leaves" with fewer marks than you expected?

**Why it works:** The viewer's eye fills in patterns. Letting it do the work keeps drawings fresh, and quick enough to finish.

## Part 6: Putting it together

### S6.1 Thumbnails and framing
**You'll learn:** to plan a drawing in tiny boxes before starting.

**The idea:** A **thumbnail** is a stamp-sized sketch (about 5 × 4 cm) that tries out what to include, where to put the horizon, and what the main subject is. It takes a minute, and it saves the big drawing.

**Do this:**
1. Draw three small frames.
2. In each, try a different view: horizon high or low, subject left or centred, close or far.
3. Use only envelopes and 2–3 values.
4. Pick the one that looks most interesting. Put the main subject off-centre, and avoid a horizon cutting the page exactly in half.

**Practice page:** rows of thumbnail frames, with prompts ("same diner, three viewpoints").

**Draw from life:** three thumbnails of the view from your window.
- *Reference:* any generated scene. Re-frame it three ways.

**Check yourself:** Is it clear in one glance what the drawing is about?

**Why it works:** Decisions are cheap when the drawing is small. Composition problems are hard to fix after an hour of detail.

### S6.2 The four-pass drawing
**You'll learn:** a reliable order for any observational drawing.

**The idea:** Every drawing in this book follows the same four passes, from light and simple to dark and detailed:

| Pass | What you do | Lessons |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Envelope** | Size and place the whole subject with a few light straight lines | S3.1 |
| **2. Shapes** | Block in the big shapes; check proportions and alignments | S2.3, S3.2, S3.3 |
| **3. Structure** | Fix perspective, ellipses and edges; erase or lighten what's wrong | S4.1–S4.5 |
| **4. Light and line** | Shadow shapes, value, texture; darker lines in front and in shadow, lighter lines in the distance and the light | S5.1–S5.4 |

**Do this:** Draw one object (a mug, a shoe, a lamp) in four separate boxes, one pass per box, so you can see the drawing grow.

**Practice page:** a 4-panel strip, repeated for 3 objects.

**Draw from life:** a coffee cup on a table, by the window.
- *Reference:* `diner` (one stool and its regular), `farmhouse` (just the house).

**Check yourself:** Did you resist darkening anything until pass 4?

**Why it works:** Working from big to small and light to dark means every mistake is caught while it's still faint and easy to fix.

### S6.3 Field sketching: people, time limits, notes
**You'll learn:** to sketch real scenes quickly, including people who won't hold still.

**The idea:**
- In a café, mall or park, nothing waits for you.
- **Time limits** force you to capture the big shapes first.
- **People** are simple shapes plus a gesture line: an oval head, a soft box for the body, lines for limbs.
- A short **written note** (time, place, one thing you noticed) makes a sketchbook page a memory.

**Do this:**
1. Set a timer: 2 minutes, then 5, then 10. Same scene each time.
2. 2 minutes: envelope and big shapes only. 5 minutes: add structure. 10 minutes: add a few values.
3. For people:
   - draw one line for the spine's curve
   - then an oval head and a box body
   - then lines for arms and legs
   - if they move, start another one, since a page of half-figures is still great practice
4. Add a field note in your best block print: time, place, one observation.

**Practice page:**
- timed boxes (2 / 5 / 10 min)
- a "people in 30 seconds" grid
- a note line under each sketch, with print guide lines

**Draw from life:**
- a café counter, a bus stop, a mall bench, the view from a parked car
- *Reference:* `diner`, `cityStreet`, `buildingFront`. The generated sketches show the style, notes included.

**Check yourself:** In the 2-minute sketch, is the main shape right? That's all it needs.

**Common slips:** spending the 2 minutes on one face. Fix: whole scene first, every time.

**Why it works:** Short, frequent sketches build seeing speed. The habit of looking is more valuable than any single finished drawing.

### S6.4 Capstone: a scene from life
**You'll learn:** to bring everything together in one finished drawing of a real place.

**Do this:**
1. Choose a scene: a diner counter, a street corner, a farmhouse, a lake shore, or your own kitchen.
2. Make three thumbnails (S6.1), and choose one.
3. Draw it with the four passes (S6.2), using:
   - perspective (Part 4) for buildings and furniture
   - shapes and negative space (Part 2) for objects and plants
   - simple-shape people (S6.3) if there are any
4. Finish with a field note.
5. Turn back to your **day-one cup drawings** (S0.1). Draw the cup again, from life, and put it next to them.

**Check yourself:**
- the four-pass checklist
- one alignment check (S3.3)
- one perspective check (S4.3/4.4)
- one value check: squint (S5.3)

**Why it works:** Seeing the day-one and final drawings side by side shows how much your *looking* has changed, which is the real skill this book teaches.
