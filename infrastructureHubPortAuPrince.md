# Site Adaptation: Port-au-Prince, Haiti

Companion to `infrastructureHubBlueprint.md`, `infrastructureHubValidation.md`, and `infrastructureHubRecommendations.md`. This file adapts the viability recommendations to a theoretical build in Port-au-Prince. Site facts are current to October 2026 where sources allow, and each is sourced at the end.

**Bottom line:** Port-au-Prince matches the blueprint's geography (a bay city under a mountain massif), but the obvious ridge is the wrong one. The engineering can be solved. The hard constraints are security and the size of the grid. So the build starts with the non-nuclear systems, which pay off on their own and also lay the groundwork for the reactor.

---

## 1. The site at a glance

| Factor | Port-au-Prince today | What it changes |
|---|---|---|
| **Population** | About 3 million in the metro area (2024) | Every load figure grows about 6x from the 500,000-person example |
| **Faults** | The Enriquillo–Plantain Garden fault runs about 5 km (3 miles) south of the city center, along the front of the Massif de la Selle. The 2010 quake released little or none of its stored strain. There are also active thrust faults under the Cul-de-Sac plain and at the southern front of the Matheux range. | Both ranges flanking the city carry active faults, so reactor siting needs a full investigation |
| **Ground** | The city sits on an alluvial plain. The port's main pier collapsed in 2010 when its reclaimed ground liquefied. Shaking was amplified on the Pétion-Ville ridge. | Nothing critical goes on the waterfront fill or on ridge crests |
| **Water** | About 65% of the city's piped water comes from 15 springs and 2 tunnels in the Massif de la Selle limestone aquifer. About 35% comes from wells in the Cul-de-Sac plain, where saltwater is seeping in. | The Massif de la Selle is the city's water tower and must be protected |
| **Power** | Électricité d'Haïti (EDH) supplied the metro area 4–6 hours a day (2022), and about 57% of the energy it generated was lost. Older figures put available capacity at about 244 MW. There was severe rationing in November 2025. | Today's grid is too small to take a reactor module |

| **Solid waste** | About 2,450 t a day, roughly 74% organic and 73% moisture. Truitier is the only controlled landfill. | The waste is too wet to burn well, which changes the waste plan |

| **Governance** | The Transitional Presidential Council's mandate ended on 7 February 2026. An interim executive is led by the Prime Minister. Election dates have slipped, and sources conflict on the current date. | Lessons for the Multi-Key Council (Section 5) |
| **Safeguards** | Haiti is a party to the NPT. Its safeguards agreement has been in force since 2006 with a Small Quantities Protocol. Whether it has an Additional Protocol is unconfirmed. | The legal framework must be upgraded before any reactor arrives |

## 2. The key finding: the Massif de la Selle is the wrong ridge

The blueprint places its "Geographical Throne" on the mountain ridge above the city. In Port-au-Prince that ridge is the Massif de la Selle, and it is ruled out three times over:

1. **Fault.** The Enriquillo–Plantain Garden fault runs along its foot, still loaded with strain. IAEA siting guidance (SSG-9) treats a capable fault at a site as disqualifying.
2. **Water.** Its limestone aquifer supplies about two-thirds of the city's piped water. A reactor, waste processing, or fuel storage inside it would put the city's drinking water at risk.
3. **Ridge shaking.** Pétion-Ville's ridge shook harder than the valley in 2010. Researchers attribute this to the ridge's shape, possibly combined with the soil layers.

**The Massif's new role is the city's water tower,** not a citadel:
- Spring protection zones.
- Reforestation to restore recharge.
- Lined storage tanks on the lower slopes for gravity distribution.

## 3. Recommended layout: a split hub joined by the rail spine

### North Campus: the Matheux corridor (Titanyen and Morne à Cabri, along Route Nationale 1)

- **Processing Yard.** Expand the existing Morne à Cabri treatment site with digesters, composting, sludge handling, a sorting line, and the rail freight terminal.
- **Desalination** on the North coast, with an offshore intake well away from the canal outfalls that drain the city into the inner bay.
- **Reactor complex: a candidate site only.** Active thrust faults are mapped at the Matheux range's southern front. Any reactor site must be set back into the range and pass a full IAEA SSG-9 fault investigation plus a karst survey.
  - **If no site passes,** put the reactor complex elsewhere in Haiti and bring the power in by transmission line. The hub's other functions don't need the reactor beneath them.
- **Data center** inside the reactor complex's secure perimeter, or underground in the Matheux range on its own if the reactor sits elsewhere.
- **A sea jetty for fuel delivery,** so nuclear fuel never travels through the city by road.
- **Community constraints:**
  - The Titanyen memorial grounds (mass graves from 2010) must be respected and kept out of the footprint.
  - Canaan, the large settlement that grew after 2010, sits nearby. That shapes the emergency planning zone and requires community consent.

### City Campus

- **Base Hospital** rebuilt on firm ground, away from ridge crests and reclaimed waterfront, and base-isolated against earthquakes.
  - **Radiotherapy with linear accelerators,** not cobalt-60 machines. A linear accelerator is electric, so there is no high-activity radioactive source to steal. It also needs the stable power the hub will provide.
  - A cyclotron for PET scans.
  - Nuclear medicine using isotopes shipped from the North Campus research reactor, once it exists.
- **Comprehensive College,** in partnership with the Université d'État d'Haïti, with a nuclear training annex at the North Campus.
- **Station clinics** with telehealth, as in the blueprint.

## 4. Systems adjusted for Port-au-Prince

### Power: grow the grid before the reactor
- **The 10% rule rules out modules on today's grid.** With about 250 MW of usable capacity, the largest single unit the grid could safely carry is about 25 MW. A 50–80 MWe module would be a third of the grid.
- **First, fix and grow the grid:**
  - Cut the 57% losses with metering and network repair.
  - Build a transmission ring.
  - Install solar and battery microgrids at stations, hospitals, and pumping plants.
- **Target size:** about 3 million people at roughly 1,000 kWh per person per year comes to about 340 MW average. Add the hub's own loads and the grid reaches roughly 450–500 MW average and 650–750 MW peak. At that size, 8–10 modules of 60–80 MWe fit within the 10% rule, backed by storage.
- **Nuclear fuel solves a Port-au-Prince problem.** Thermal plants and hospital generators run on diesel and heavy fuel oil trucked through contested roads, which made the 2022 Varreux blockade so effective. A reactor module refuels about every two years, by sea.

### Water
- **Demand:** 3 million people at 50–100 L per person per day is 150,000–300,000 m³ a day.
- **Sources:**
  - Protect the Massif springs.
  - Cut pumping in the Cul-de-Sac plain to stop saltwater intrusion.
  - Add 100,000–200,000 m³ a day of shore-based reverse-osmosis desalination, which draws about 15–30 MW on average.
- **Stations double as water kiosks.** Many residents already buy water from kiosks and trucks. Each station "fortress" holds two to three days of storage.

### Sanitation: formalize the night shift that already exists
The blueprint's "sealed, airtight pods" carried at night already exist in Port-au-Prince at small scale. *Bayakou* empty pits at night, and SOIL collects sealed toilet containers every week.

- **Scale up sealed-container toilets** (the pod at household scale), with swap and transfer points at stations.
- **Night rail to the Processing Yard,** where anaerobic digestion makes biogas and thermophilic composting makes compost. **The compost feeds the reforestation of the Massif de la Selle,** which protects the springs and closes the loop.
- **Professionalize the bayakou trade:** protective equipment, mechanical emptying, fair wages, and an apprenticeship track at the college.
- **Cholera control is the first measurable public-health win.**
- **Don't build a citywide sewer network** for now. It would cost a great deal, use scarce water, and take decades. Reserve sewers for dense new districts.

### Solid waste: compost first, not burning
- **Waste this wet burns poorly.** At about 74% organic and 73% moisture, the mass-burn plant from the general recommendations doesn't suit Port-au-Prince.
- **Organics** go to digestion and composting.
- **Dry recyclables are exchanged for credits.** Plastic Bank already pays Haitian collectors in digital credits for plastic, a working version of the blueprint's "refuse drop-offs into credits."
- **The residual dry fraction** becomes refuse-derived fuel for a small waste-to-energy unit or a cement kiln.
- **Upgrade Truitier** to a sanitary landfill.
- **Clearing the canals of garbage also reduces flooding.**
- **Freight volume:** about 2,450 t a day of waste plus on the order of 1,000 t a day of excreta and sludge, so about 3,500 t a night. Plan for full freight trains on several lines, fed by trucks, not single tram cars.

### Rail spine routes
- **North, along Route Nationale 1, to the North Campus.** This is the critical corridor for freight, power, and fuel.
- **West, along Route Nationale 2,** to Carrefour and Léogâne.
- **East** through Delmas to the airport, Croix-des-Bouquets, and the Cul-de-Sac plain.
- **Cable cars for the hills.** Light rail can't climb the roughly 400 m to Pétion-Ville in a few kilometres. Medellín's Metrocable and La Paz's Mi Teleférico are the precedents for steep, informal cities.
- **Structure:**
  - Deep piles through the alluvium and reclaimed fill.
  - Seismic design for both the strike-slip fault and the thrust faults.
  - Hurricane wind loading.
- **Bring tap-taps in as feeder routes.** Their livelihoods depend on it, and so does the line's ridership.

## 5. Security and governance

### The Utility Valve already exists, held by armed groups
In 2022 the G9 gang federation dug trenches across the entrance to the Varreux fuel terminal. Hospitals shut or cut services because they couldn't run their generators, and water pumps stopped. The blueprint imagines the hub's operators holding this lever; in Port-au-Prince, whoever captures a chokepoint holds it.

**Design rule: the city must survive the capture of any single node.**
- Station reserves (water, batteries, spare sanitation containers).
- Hospitals and clinics with their own solar and battery systems.
- More than one feed to every critical load.
- No dependence on a single fuel terminal.

### A hard nuclear gate
No nuclear fuel or high-activity radioactive source arrives until the state can show three things:
1. It controls the site, the transport routes, and the port of entry.
2. It meets IAEA physical protection standards (INFCIRC/225) against a threat that includes armed groups able to ambush armored vehicles, as happened on 18 October 2026.
3. It has updated its safeguards. A Small Quantities Protocol doesn't apply once a state has a reactor, so it must be rescinded, and an Additional Protocol concluded. It also needs an independent nuclear regulator with authority over the reactor.

**Under today's conditions, the nuclear phases can't proceed.** The non-nuclear phases are worth building anyway, and they create the security, institutions, and grid the reactor will need.

### The Multi-Key Council, informed by Haiti's own experience
Haiti's 2024–2026 Transitional Presidential Council shows how a multi-party council can split. Days before its mandate ended, five of its nine members voted to remove the Prime Minister. Lessons for the hub's council:
- **Threshold signing** (for example, 3 of 4) rather than unanimity.
- **Keys held by institutions that outlast political transitions:**
  - The engineers' body.
  - The medical board.
  - The student union.
  - Neighborhood representatives.
- **Rules for replacing a key-holder, agreed in advance.**
- **No key can touch reactor safety or the lifeline floors.**
- **Kreyòl first** in every public interface and log.
- **Formal seats for the people whose work the hub absorbs:** bayakou associations, tap-tap associations, and water vendors.

## 6. Build order for Port-au-Prince

1. **Phase 0, the preconditions.**
   - Corridor security.
   - A nuclear regulator.
   - Safeguards upgrades.
   - Land and community agreements, including Titanyen and Canaan.
2. **Phase 1, steps that work even under insecurity.**
   - Station kiosks and solar microgrids.
   - Scaled-up container sanitation.
   - Expanding Morne à Cabri.
   - Organics processing.
   - Cutting grid losses.
   - Protecting the Massif watershed.
3. **Phase 2, the northern build-out.**
   - The North rail spine along Route Nationale 1.
   - The Processing Yard.
   - Coastal desalination, powered by solar, batteries, and existing generation.
4. **Phase 3, the City Campus.** The hospital with linear accelerators and the college. The first nuclear operators train abroad.
5. **Phase 4, the research reactor** at the North Campus once the nuclear gate is passed: isotopes for the hospital and training for the college.
6. **Phase 5, power modules,** added as the grid grows toward 650–750 MW peak.
7. **Phase 6, a second hub** in another Haitian city, under the open-source mandate.
