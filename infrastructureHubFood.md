# Food: Feeding the City and Growing More of It

Companion to `infrastructureHubSupplyChain.md`, `infrastructureHubPortAuPrince.md`, and `infrastructureHubEconomics.md`.

**Bottom line:**
- **Haiti is in a hunger crisis now.**
  - About 5.8 million people (52% of the population) were projected to be in IPC Phase 3 or worse (crisis or emergency hunger) for March–June 2026.
  - In October 2026 the UN warned that 6.5 million (57%) could face acute food insecurity by March 2027.

  The hub can't wait for new farms to produce. Its first job is keeping food moving and getting it to people.
- **The city will import much of its staple food for a long time.** About 80% of Haiti's rice is imported. The Artibonite Valley grew most of the country's own rice, but gangs seized its irrigation canals and output has fallen by at least 48%. The goal is resilience and a rising local share, not self-sufficiency.
- **The hub's best food levers are things it is already building,** not high-tech farms:
  - Free power for cold storage (Haiti loses 30–40% of its mangoes after harvest).
  - Compost from the sanitation system.
  - Water.
  - Secure freight.
  - Guaranteed demand through school meals.
- **Food must never become a Utility Valve.** Food is never conditional on anything.

---

## 1. Where the city's food comes from today

- **Imports:**
  - Rice (about 80% of what's eaten), wheat flour, and cooking oil, through the port and across the Dominican border.
  - Haiti cut its rice tariff to 3% in 1995, opening the market to US rice. Bill Clinton later called that policy a mistake.
  - Rice supplies about 23% of daily calories.
- **Domestic production:**
  - Rice from the Artibonite Valley.
  - Crops from the Cul-de-Sac plain.
  - Maize, beans, sorghum, root crops, plantain, breadfruit, and mango from the hills.
  - Fish from small-scale fishers.
- **Distribution:**
  - The *Madan Sara*, women traders who carry produce from the countryside to city markets, keep the food system running.
  - They face extortion on roads controlled by gangs.
- **Losses:**
  - 30–40% of mangoes are lost after harvest.
  - Estimates for the wider produce chain run as high as 50–60%, mostly from poor roads and no cold storage.
- **Planning numbers for 3 million people:**
  - **Grain:** about 900 tonnes a day, assuming roughly half of a 2,100-calorie daily diet comes from grains.
  - **Food reserve:** three months of staple grains comes to about 80,000 tonnes.

## 2. First: keep food moving and reaching people

- **Free school meals** at schools near stations and at resource centers.
  - Food is bought locally through Haiti's national homegrown school-meals model. WFP already buys from local farmers, putting more than $1 million a month into the economy, and the national policy targets full coverage by 2030.
  - The meals are a free service, funded like free energy.
- **Treating malnutrition at clinics** with therapeutic food made in Haiti. Meds & Food for Kids already produces it in Cap-Haïtien.
- **A food reserve:**
  - Three months of staple grains in airtight storage at the North Campus, plus smaller stocks at stations.
  - The stock rotates through the school meals so it never spoils.
- **No conditions, ever.**
  - The blueprint's "Tokenized Economy" ties access to civic-compliance credits. That never applies to food, water, or healthcare.
  - Food aid stays neutral, like the clinics.

## 3. Moving food: the rail spine's day shift

- **The day shift carries food** from the North Campus jetty and from farms on the Cul-de-Sac plain and to the north, to markets at the stations.
- **Room for the Madan Sara:**
  - Space on freight trains, free or at cost.
  - Lockable stalls and cold storage at stations.
  - Safer than the roads.
- **Food and waste never mix.** This is non-negotiable for preventing cholera.
  - Food never travels in sanitation cars or waste pods.
  - Separate cars, loading docks, and schedules.
  - Cars are washed down and inspected.
- **A later "food line."** Extend the Route Nationale 1 line north toward Saint-Marc and the Artibonite Valley once that corridor is secure, connecting the rice bowl to the city.

## 4. Cutting losses: the cheapest new food

- **Cold rooms** at stations and rural collection points, running on solar and free power.
- **Ice for fishers.**
- **Plastic field crates.** In one Haitian study, crates cut damage to mangoes on the way to collection centers from 8.25% to 3.10%.
- **Small-scale processing:** solar dryers, mills, and simple food processing (peanut butter, cassava flour, fruit pulp). This also creates jobs and adds value.
- **Controlling aflatoxin,** a toxic mold on peanuts and maize, through proper drying and storage. Meds & Food for Kids is the Haitian precedent.

## 5. Growing more near the city

- **Compost from the Processing Yard and fertilizer from urine.** Hot composting makes human waste safe for farming under WHO guidelines. SOIL already does this in Haiti.
- **Tree crops on the Massif de la Selle:** breadfruit, mango, avocado, and plantain. They provide food, reforest the slopes, and protect the springs, the same land the water plan already protects.
- **Farms on the edge of the city,** on the Cul-de-Sac plain and along the North Campus corridor.
  - Drip irrigation using harvested rainwater and treated water.
  - **No desalinated water for staple crops.** Even with free power, membranes and upkeep make it too expensive per calorie.
- **Greenhouses** for leafy greens, herbs, seedlings, and stock for the tree nurseries. They are not a source of staple calories.
- **Fish:** ice and cold storage for fishers, plus fish ponds (tilapia) where water allows.
- **The Artibonite Valley.** Gangs have captured its irrigation canals and charge farmers for each watering. Returning the canals to farmers is a security priority under the mandate to protect critical infrastructure: food security is security.

## 6. Fertilizer, seeds, and research

- **Fertilizer:** mostly imported for now, with compost and urine fertilizer made locally. An optional later step is a small fertilizer (ammonia) plant running on reactor power.
- **Seeds:**
  - Support Haiti's local seed systems.
  - Build a **Haitian seed bank** of local varieties, with duplicate copies stored abroad. This is the library principle (copies elsewhere) applied to seeds; the Svalbard Global Seed Vault is the precedent.
- **Research.** Agronomy and food science join the College's science track:
  - Crop varieties that tolerate heat, drought, and salt.
  - Better systems for handling food after harvest.
  - Both are expertise other tropical cities can use.

## 7. Policy: what the hub does and doesn't do

- **The hub doesn't raise food prices.**
  - Protective tariffs would raise prices on staples that the urban poor depend on to survive.
  - Haiti's 2018 fuel-price riots show how dangerous price shocks are.
  - Trade policy belongs to the state.
- **The hub does:**
  - Lower costs: cold storage, transport, compost, and water.
  - Guarantee demand: school meals and hub canteens buy local, with every purchase published on the ledger.
  - Protect farmland and irrigation canals.
  - Train farmers.

## 8. What stays imported

- Most rice and all wheat.
- Cooking oil.
- Most fertilizer and some seeds.
- Packaging.
- Refrigeration compressors and drip-irrigation equipment.

**Track the share of school and hub meals bought locally,** and raise it over time.

## 9. Measures of success

- Number of people in IPC Phase 3 or worse in the hub's areas.
- Acute child malnutrition seen at station clinics.
- Share of school and hub meals bought locally.
- Food losses at collection points.
- Days of reserve food on hand.
- Local food prices compared with the cost of imports.

## 10. Build order

- **Phases 0–1:**
  - School meals.
  - Malnutrition treatment at clinics.
  - The food reserve.
  - Cold rooms at the first stations.
  - Composting begins.
- **Phase 2:**
  - Day-shift food freight and space for the Madan Sara.
  - Food warehouses at the North Campus.
  - Farms on the edge of the city.
- **Phases 3–4:**
  - Scale up tree crops.
  - Build the seed bank.
  - Start agronomy research.
- **Phase 5 and later:**
  - The optional fertilizer plant.
  - The northern line toward the Artibonite, once the corridor is secure.
