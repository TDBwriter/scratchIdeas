# The Criminal Economy: How Haiti's Gangs Pay Their Way

Companion to `infrastructureHubStabilization.md` and `infrastructureHubEconomics.md`. This file sets out what the best public estimates say Haiti's armed groups earn, how that business stays viable, and which parts of the hub's design cut off each revenue stream without firing a shot.

**Bottom line:**
- **The money is smaller than the damage.** The International Crisis Group estimated in late 2025 that the Viv Ansanm gang coalition brings in **$60–75M a year**, mostly from illegal road tolls and fees on cargo coming from the Dominican Republic.
  - That is roughly the cost of the hub's free-energy allowance.
  - Meanwhile, Haiti's economy shrank for a seventh straight year in 2025, by about 2.7–3.1%.
  - Gangs capture a small slice of the economy while destroying much more of it.
- **The business model is control of chokepoints, not wealth.** Holding a road, a port gate, or a canal turns into steady, low-cost revenue. Most of it is extortion on movement.
- **What keeps it running:**
  - Political and business patrons.
  - Cheap weapons from the US.
  - A steady supply of desperate young recruits.
  - Near-total impunity.
- **The hub's strongest non-violent lever is to route around the chokepoints and give recruits something better.** Its biggest risk is becoming the richest chokepoint in the country, so **the hub never pays armed groups, ever.**

---

## 1. Where the money comes from

| Stream | What the estimates say |
|---|---|
| **Road tolls and cargo fees** | The largest source. GI-TOC (the Global Initiative Against Transnational Organized Crime) reported in February 2024 that gangs collect roughly **$6,000–8,000 a day** from drivers on the national highways. Crisis Group's **$60–75M a year** for Viv Ansanm comes mostly from tolls and fees on Dominican cargo. |
| **Business extortion** | GI-TOC found businesses paying **up to $20,000 a week**. |
| **Kidnapping for ransom** | Demands run from hundreds of thousands to millions of dollars, but actual payments are often far lower and are funded by borrowing from family and friends. Members of the diaspora are targeted because families abroad are presumed able to pay more. |
| **Patronage** | The UN Panel of Experts' September 2026 report describes political, economic, and security figures backing gangs when it suits them. It accuses a former mayor of diverting embezzled public money to Viv Ansanm leaders and laundering the rest through local businesses. It accuses a former legislator of supplying weapons and money; that person denies it. |
| **Capturing essential services** | Gangs charge Artibonite farmers each time they water their fields, and they have used control of fuel, water, and aid as leverage. The 2022 blockade of the Varreux fuel terminal is the clearest case. |
| **Arms trafficking** | A handgun bought in the US for $400–500 can resell in Haiti for up to $10,000, according to UNODC (2023). Weapons mainly arrive from Florida, some through the Dominican Republic. Close to a million rounds of ammunition traced to Dominican police stocks turned up in 2024. Up to 500,000 illegal guns are estimated to be in circulation. |

All of these figures are estimates with gaps. There is no verified total for kidnapping revenue, and the numbers come from different years.

## 2. Why the business stays viable

1. **Chokepoints pay steadily.** A checkpoint on a highway, a port gate, or an irrigation canal earns every day and costs little to hold.
2. **Patrons protect it.** The UN Panel describes elites who fund and arm gangs for their own ends. Sanctions have little bite: the Panel found **$0 frozen** in Haiti's central bank for sanctioned gang leaders.
3. **Labor is cheap.** The UN estimates 30–50% of armed-group members are children, and recruitment tripled in 2025. With seven years of economic contraction, 28% inflation in 2025, about 1.45 million people displaced, and more than half the country going hungry, joining costs a young person very little.
4. **Guns are cheap at the source.** The large markup between US and Haitian prices makes arms trafficking a profitable business in its own right.
5. **There are no consequences.** Courts, prisons, and asset freezes barely work; the 2024 mass prison break is the clearest example.

**Weak points:**
- The revenue depends on traffic passing through gang territory.
- Coalitions fracture. The Panel reports Viv Ansanm splintering in 2026, with hundreds killed in fighting in Cité Soleil and on the Cul-de-Sac plain.
- As the economy shrinks, so does the base gangs can extort.

## 3. Cutting off each stream through design

| Revenue stream | What the hub does |
|---|---|
| **Road tolls** | The rail spine and sea routes from the North Campus jetty carry people and freight **around** the toll roads. Electric vehicles running on free charging have no fuel supply for anyone to blockade. |
| **Fees on Dominican cargo** | Sea delivery to the North Campus jetty replaces the land route through gang territory. |
| **Business extortion** | **The hub never pays protection money or "taxes" to armed groups,** including at construction sites, on supply runs, and through contractors. Every procurement is published on the ledger, so hidden payments show up. |
| **Kidnapping** | Fewer people need to travel through gang-held ground: stations are secured nodes, the rail is protected, and services sit close to home. |
| **Capturing essential services** | Free lifeline water and energy, plus reserves at every station, make capturing any single node much less valuable. Returning the Artibonite canals to farmers removes a toll on food. |
| **Patronage and laundering** | Transparent customs manifests, published procurement, and checks on every supplier. Financial investigations and asset freezes belong to the state; the hub's records make them easier. |
| **Recruitment** | Apprenticeships, school meals, paid community work, and the resource centers' exit paths compete directly for the same young people. |
| **Arms** | The hub's ships are fully transparent and scanned at both ends, so they can never carry weapons. Stopping the flow from Florida is a matter for US and regional enforcement. |

## 4. The hub as a target

The hub will be the richest prize in Haiti: construction budgets, jobs, contracts, a port, ships, and free power. Gangs and their patrons will try to tax it or capture it.

**Defenses:**
- **A no-payment rule with no exceptions.** If the hub pays once, it becomes a gang revenue source.
- **Everything visible:** procurement, customs manifests, and payroll totals published on the ledger.
- **Rotating suppliers and contractors,** so no single one becomes a channel for extortion.
- **Site security** under the stabilization model, with Response Teams guarding only critical infrastructure.
- **Community ownership.** Services that neighborhoods depend on and help run are harder to capture and costlier to attack.

## 5. Targeting the instigators without force

**Starting point:** most gang members are followers, and 30–50% are children. The leverage lies with the instigators: gang leaders, financiers, and political and business patrons. That leverage can be applied without violence.

### Tools that already exist
- **UN sanctions,** renewed in October 2026 (Resolution 2831). They impose an asset freeze, a travel ban, and an arms embargo on 9 individuals and 2 groups. A new criterion covers embezzlement and diversion of public funds, which **for the first time reaches corrupt patrons, not just gunmen.**
- **US terrorist designations.** Since May 2025, the US has designated Viv Ansanm and Gran Grif as Foreign Terrorist Organizations.
  - Giving them "material support" is a federal crime.
  - Doing business with them risks secondary sanctions.
  - This hardens the hub's no-payment rule: a toll paid to a designated group could expose the hub and its contractors to US terrorism-financing law.
- **US prosecutions.** Germine Joly, the former leader of the 400 Mawozo gang, received 35 years in prison (2024) for smuggling US guns paid for with laundered ransom money. He then received a life sentence (December 2025) for the 2021 kidnapping of US missionaries.

### Why freezes have caught so little so far
- **Only two sanctioned individuals have had accounts frozen,** and the sums were small (UN Panel, 2026).
- **Gangs have become financially self-sufficient.** In 2024, GI-TOC found them funding guns and fighters from tolls, extortion, and kidnapping, the last estimated at about $25M a year. Leaders hold cash locally and have little need for banks or foreign travel. A UN expert says some gangs no longer need elite money at all.

So freezing bank accounts alone catches little. **The pressure has to land where the cash must enter the formal system:**
1. **Guns and ammunition.** These are bought abroad with dollars and need financiers to move the money and the shipments, as the Joly case shows. US and Dominican enforcement can target the buying networks.
2. **Patrons' assets:** businesses, property, and accounts, in Haiti and in Florida, the Dominican Republic, and Canada. The new corruption criterion and foreign sanctions reach them, and foreign courts can seize assets held in their jurisdictions.
3. **Laundering through businesses.** The hub's published procurement, supplier checks, and customs manifests make laundering through hub contracts visible.
4. **Ransom flows,** which are often wired from abroad. Financial intelligence units can trace them to the kidnappers. **Families paying to free relatives are never the target;** US policy since 2015 has been not to prosecute them.
5. **Big cash purchases** of property, vehicles, and businesses, flagged under reporting thresholds.

### Recapturing cash
- **Only accountable financial police and customs seize cash,** under court orders and with documented custody at every step. **Response Teams never seize cash.** When armed units take money, it disappears, and security work turns into looting.
- **Every seizure is published on the ledger:** amount, date, and legal basis.
- **Forfeited money goes, by court order, to victims and to programs in the affected neighborhoods,** also tracked on the ledger.

### Due process
- **Knowing who the instigators are is not enough.** Designations and seizures need evidence that holds up.
- **The hub supplies records, not verdicts.**
- **The Multi-Key Council has no role in choosing targets.** Otherwise financial pressure becomes a political weapon, another form of the Utility Valve.

### The four-part approach
| Who | What works |
|---|---|
| **Followers** | Jobs, apprenticeships, and exit paths through the resource centers |
| **Leaders** | A legal choice: prosecution, or surrender on terms the state sets |
| **Financiers and patrons** | Sanctions, asset seizures, and prosecution at home and abroad |
| **The revenue itself** | Route around the chokepoints and make no payments |
